Group '1' represents a segment of French participants best characterized as **Young Ethical Pragmatists**. This group is distinctly younger, predominantly composed of students (86.73% aged 18-29, 67.26% students, both significantly more frequent than the full sample). Their profile reveals a strong commitment to ethical and environmental principles, coupled with a pragmatic and nuanced stance on how these principles should be implemented, often showing resistance to singular authorities, rigid mandates, and blanket bans.

The central interpretive pattern for this group is their **strong ethical and environmental consciousness**. They display a very high level of acceptance, often "Totally acceptable," for propositions that align with social justice and ecological sustainability. For instance, they are significantly more likely to find it "Totally acceptable" to "Ban the import of food whose production does not respect human rights" (73.01% vs 54.62%), "Redistribute the world’s food and water resources fairly" (74.78% acceptable/totally acceptable vs 52.88%), "Impose optimized and reasoned energy management" (75.22% acceptable/totally acceptable vs 56.03%), and "Impose surplus food to be donated to disadvantaged populations" (85.40% acceptable/totally acceptable vs 67.89%). They also show less unacceptability towards prioritizing biodiversity over yield and are more likely to find universal waste recycling "Totally acceptable." This commitment extends to supporting aid programs for disadvantaged populations and banning meat from animals whose welfare was not respected.

However, their ethical idealism is tempered by a **skepticism towards centralized, singular authority and prescriptive mandates**. They are significantly more likely to find it "Unacceptable" or "Totally unacceptable" for any single entity – be it "local authorities alone," "communities alone," "the State alone," or "a citizens' council without which no decisions could be taken" – to decide food policies. This suggests a preference for collaborative or multi-stakeholder governance models over any form of singular control. This caution against rigidity is further evidenced by their strong rejection of "Follow policies defined for a minimum of 10 years" (47.79% "Totally unacceptable").

This pragmatic approach also extends to specific food system interventions. While supportive of general goals, they are less enthusiastic about *systematically* favoring specific solutions like "small producers and family farms" or "certified products," often showing higher neutrality. They also tend to reject outright bans or mandatory actions that might limit personal choice or impose obligations: they are more likely to find bans on "ultra-processed products" or "too fatty and/or too sweet" products "Unacceptable," and are more rejecting of "Impose a civic service" or "Create urban gardens that citizens would be obligated to maintain." Their increased unacceptability towards "Remove all synthetic chemicals from agriculture" further underscores a pragmatic, rather than absolutist, environmental perspective.

Furthermore, this group demonstrates a **science-oriented and personally responsible outlook**. They are notably less likely to distrust science regarding diet and show cautious openness to trusting science for innovative technologies. They are also less likely to agree with statements like "Eating is a pleasure and I don’t want to compromise on that" or "I consider that it’s those who pollute the most who should make efforts, not me," indicating a willingness to make personal efforts and accept responsibility. Their stronger disagreement with "I think that individual actions alone won’t change anything" reinforces their belief in personal agency. They also show a more egalitarian perspective, being less likely to agree that "eating well should be earned" and more strongly disagreeing that "promoting cultural diversity is promoting communalism," indicating an open and inclusive social stance.

A minor ambiguity arises in their stance on "Support, through taxes, food companies that commit to sustainable food," which they find more "Unacceptable" and less "Neither acceptable nor unacceptable." This contrasts with their general pro-sustainability and pro-aid stance, possibly indicating a specific reservation about corporate subsidies versus direct support for people or broader regulatory frameworks.

In conclusion, Group '1' is a clearly defined and coherent group. They are **Young Ethical Pragmatists (Group 1)**: a generation characterized by strong ethical and environmental values, a deep belief in individual responsibility and the power of science, yet wary of rigid, top-down, or singular authority-driven solutions in favor of more adaptable, collaborative, and less prescriptive approaches.
