Group '2': The Skeptical Localists

Group 2 exhibits a distinct profile characterized by a **strong skepticism towards centralized authority and broad interventions**, coupled with a clear **preference for localism, tradition, and smaller-scale, ethically-driven food systems**. This group is older than average, with a higher proportion of individuals aged 50-64, married individuals, and professionals/executives or retired individuals, and fewer students. This demographic context likely contributes to their pragmatic and discerning outlook.

A central theme for this group is their **distrust of top-down control and broad mandates**. They are significantly more likely to find both national and local state involvement in food policy *alone* "totally unacceptable." This general distrust extends to government at both national and local levels, where "Totally agree" with not trusting government is more frequent. Crucially, they also express considerable unacceptability for "Trust science in the development of innovative technologies" and are less likely to totally disagree with the statement "I don’t trust science when it comes to my diet." This indicates a fundamental reservation towards perceived expert-driven or centrally dictated solutions. This skepticism is further evidenced by their increased unacceptability of "Follow policies defined for a minimum of 10 years," suggesting a preference for flexibility or shorter-term responsiveness rather than rigid, long-term plans. In contrast, they show more openness to "Have a citizens' council without which no decisions could be taken" and find "Impose civic education courses" more acceptable, hinting at a preference for participatory governance where citizens are informed and involved.

In terms of food systems, this group strongly advocates for **local and traditional practices**. They are significantly more supportive of "Tax food that was not produced and processed in France" and display strong enthusiasm for "Systematically favor supply from small producers and family farms." Their preference for local distinctiveness is highlighted by their strong rejection of "Regularly impose dishes from other cultures in collective catering" and their less frequent total opposition to "Impose a baseline amount of traditional local recipes in catering." They also show more acceptance for "Never sell packaged products when they can be sold in bulk," aligning with an anti-industrial, pro-local, and environmentally-conscious stance on consumption. They are also more accepting or neutral about banning ultra-processed products, indicating a preference for less processed, potentially healthier options.

While demonstrating environmental concern, their approach is nuanced and pragmatic rather than dogmatically prescriptive. They strongly prioritize "respect for biodiversity and nature over yield" and are more open to "Remove all synthetic chemicals from agriculture." They also show greater acceptance for "Ban the sale of meat from animals whose welfare was not respected." However, they are more likely to find "Only use renewable energy" unacceptable, and are notably more neutral or less enthusiastic about simply accepting mandates for "Impose optimized and reasoned energy management," "Impose optimized and reasoned water management," or "Require all players, including consumers, to recycle their waste." This suggests they value environmental goals but are cautious about sweeping or inflexible mandates, especially those seen as impractical or overly burdensome. This pragmatic stance is reinforced by their increased agreement that "it’s those who pollute the most who should make efforts, not me," indicating a tendency to place responsibility on major actors rather than individuals.

Regarding social and economic justice, Group 2 shows a selective approach. They are significantly more supportive of "Impose a fair redistribution of profits throughout the food chain." However, they are notably less enthusiastic or more neutral towards broader state-led social interventions, such as "Support, through taxes, aid programs for disadvantaged regions and populations," "Impose surplus food to be donated to disadvantaged populations," or "Redistribute the world’s food and water resources fairly." This indicates support for structural fairness within the food system itself, but a more reserved or cautious stance on extensive welfare state interventions. They also show less unacceptability for forced wage redistribution, implying some openness but no clear endorsement.

In summary, Group 2 comprises individuals who are **wary of centralized power and technological fixes, preferring a food system grounded in local production, traditional values, and ethical practices**, with a strong emphasis on biodiversity and animal welfare. They advocate for fairness within the food chain but are pragmatic and reserved regarding extensive state-led social or environmental mandates. Their profile is quite coherent, articulating a consistent viewpoint that combines a distrust of authority with a principled, yet practical, commitment to localized, responsible food systems.

**Proposed Name:** Group 2: The Skeptical Localists
