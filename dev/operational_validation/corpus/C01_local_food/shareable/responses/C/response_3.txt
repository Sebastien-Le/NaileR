Group 3 comprises individuals who demonstrate a notably pragmatic and less ideologically driven approach to sustainable food systems. They are characterized by a pronounced trust in science and innovation as solutions, coupled with a strong emphasis on personal choice, eating pleasure, and practicality in their daily lives. An overrepresentation of "Employees" within this group suggests a profile rooted in a working-life reality that values efficiency and tangible outcomes.

These individuals are generally resistant to many proposed interventions aimed at sustainability when these measures are perceived as costly, inconvenient, or as impinging on personal freedom and economic efficiency. For instance, they show significantly more resistance to prioritizing respect for biodiversity and nature over yield, imposing optimized energy management, removing all synthetic chemicals from agriculture, or exclusively using renewable energy. Similarly, they are less enthusiastic about globally redistributing food and water resources fairly and more opposed to mandatory recycling for all. This suggests a skepticism towards policies that might drastically alter current production methods or consumer habits, particularly if they perceive a negative impact on productivity or cost.

In economic matters, Group 3 is particularly resistant to protectionist measures like taxing food not produced in France and strongly opposes imposing a fair redistribution of profits throughout the food chain. They are also markedly more likely to find an economic model based on barter "totally unacceptable," indicating a clear preference for conventional market economies. However, their stance is not purely free-market; they are more likely to find selling out-of-season products at much higher prices "unacceptable" and show increased support for limiting imports of food that can be produced locally and systematically favoring supply from small producers and family farms. This nuanced position suggests a balance between market principles and a cultural or community-focused appreciation for localism and fair consumer pricing, without resorting to broad protectionist taxes. They also show a marked resistance to aid programs for disadvantaged regions funded through taxes.

Regarding social and cultural aspects, the group values nutritional education in schools and strongly supports imposing a baseline amount of traditional local recipes in catering, showing an appreciation for cultural heritage. They are also more open to cultural diversity in catering and supporting specific diets. However, they are significantly more opposed to limiting meat in collective catering, reflecting a strong belief that "eating is a pleasure" they "don’t want to compromise on." This individualistic perspective extends to a higher agreement with not wanting to give up the "diversity of the food I eat" and agreeing that they "don't have the time to improve my habits," further explaining their resistance to restrictive or demanding lifestyle changes. A notable wariness about children being "indoctrinated by school" also shapes their perspective on certain educational mandates.

In terms of governance, while less opposed to various levels of authority (local, state, communities, NGOs), they show a distinct resistance to empowering a citizens' council with veto power, preferring more structured decision-making processes.
