# Blind interpretation G1_R03

Group 1 is predominantly characterized by a young demographic, with a significant majority (86.73%) aged 18 to 29 and a high proportion of students (67.26%). They are less likely to be married but more likely to be in a relationship, which aligns with their younger age profile.

The beliefs of this group reflect a strong commitment to ethical and environmental sustainability, coupled with a pragmatic and nuanced perspective on governance and individual choice. They show overwhelming support for socially responsible food systems: they are significantly more likely to find propositions like banning food imports that disrespect human rights, fair redistribution of global food and water resources, supporting aid programs for disadvantaged populations, and imposing surplus food donation to be "Totally acceptable" or "Acceptable." Similarly, they strongly endorse environmental measures, showing high acceptance for optimized energy and water management, and prioritizing biodiversity. They are also more likely to value mandatory waste recycling and higher animal welfare standards.

However, their approach is not dogmatic or absolutist. This group demonstrates a clear aversion to overly prescriptive regulations and bans, as seen by their increased likelihood to find measures like banning all synthetic chemicals from agriculture, banning ultra-processed or fatty/sweet products, or imposing customers to prioritize "ugly" produce, as "Unacceptable." They also show more ambivalence regarding systematically favoring certified products or reducing the number of product references, suggesting a preference for consumer choice and diversity.

In terms of governance, this group expresses strong skepticism towards any single entity—whether local authorities, communities, the State, or NGOs—having sole decision-making power over food policies. They also reject long-term, inflexible policies and show reluctance towards citizens' councils with absolute veto power, indicating a preference for more collaborative, adaptable, and potentially multi-stakeholder governance models. They are also less inclined to accept mandatory civic service or obligatory urban garden maintenance, preferring voluntary engagement over imposed duties.

Their personal attitudes further reinforce this profile. They are more willing to compromise on eating for pure pleasure, believe they have the time to improve their habits, and are less likely to defer responsibility for environmental efforts solely to major polluters. Critically, they strongly believe in the impact of individual actions. This group also exhibits greater trust in science, particularly concerning diet, and is less distrustful of government or fearful of school indoctrination. They are also less likely to be disinterested in food politics, supporting cultural diversity and rejecting radical economic shifts like a barter system.

Based on this distinctive combination of strong social and environmental engagement with a pragmatic, nuanced, and autonomy-respecting stance, this group is named **The Engaged Pragmatists (Group 1)**. This name captures their proactive commitment to a sustainable and equitable food future ("Engaged") while highlighting their critical thinking, aversion to rigid mandates, and preference for balanced, adaptable solutions that respect individual freedom and scientific insights ("Pragmatists").
