# TEXTUAL V1 — approved methodological reference

**Status: APPROVED METHODOLOGICAL REFERENCE**

## Scope

This document is the approved methodological reference for the classic TEXTUAL interpretation routes:

- `nail_textual()`;
- `nail_textual_prep()`, with its distinct preparation output contract.

It does not govern `nail_textual_contextualized()`, `nail_catdes_textual()`, statistical/textual composition, or multiagent workflows.

The prompt below is reproduced verbatim from the approved TEXTUAL V1 specification. The bracketed dynamic sections are runtime substitutions, not invitations to rewrite the doctrine.

## Epistemic object

The texts are the empirical observations. Themes, interpretive frames, and coherence assessments are constructed qualitatively by the language model; they are not statistics calculated in advance by R.

# Introduction

## How to Read the Textual Evidence

The evidence below consists of textual responses associated with one group.

These texts are the empirical material for the qualitative interpretation. The thematic structure, interpretive frames, and coherence assessments are constructed by the language model; they are not statistical results calculated beforehand.

### Textual units and traceability

Each displayed text has a mechanically assigned identifier linked to a source observation.

A textual observation is not necessarily a distinct respondent. Several observations may originate from the same person, and identical wording may occur in several observations.

Preserve the original meaning of each text. Use the supplied text identifiers when referring to representative or contrasting evidence.

Do not invent quotations, change the meaning of quoted passages, or attribute statements to unidentified individuals.

### Corpus coverage and selection

The corpus information describes the available observations, non-empty responses, distinct texts, and texts selected for interpretation.

[Dynamic description of actual corpus coverage and sampling settings.]

Interpret only the texts actually displayed.

A sample of the available texts may not reproduce every theme, minority position, contradiction, or frequency pattern present in the complete corpus.

Do not treat a theme that is absent from the displayed texts as absent from the complete corpus, rejected by the group, or irrelevant to its members.

When all available texts are displayed, the interpretation still remains limited by what participants expressed and by the study's observation process.

### Recurrence, prevalence, and importance

Identify themes that recur across the displayed texts.

Distinguish the recurrence of an expression from the prevalence of an opinion among distinct individuals.

Repeated or identical texts are separate recorded occurrences but not necessarily independent respondents or independent confirmations.

A frequently repeated formulation is not automatically a group consensus.

A theme may be substantively important even when expressed by only a small number of texts.

Do not invent numerical prevalence estimates or population-level proportions from a qualitative sample.

### Literal meaning and nuanced expressions

Interpret each statement according to its actual wording and context.

Preserve negations, double negations, litotes, hesitation, conditional statements, irony when clearly supported, and differences in strength of endorsement.

Do not turn qualified acceptance into enthusiasm, a reservation into categorical rejection, or a question into an asserted belief.

Respect the distinction between perceived characteristics, expressed attitudes, stated intentions, reported experiences, and observed behaviors.

Ambiguity must be acknowledged rather than silently resolved.

### Thematic interpretation

Identify recurring ideas and organize them into meaningful themes.

Distinguish themes that share vocabulary but express different concerns, evaluations, or interpretations.

Look for broader interpretive frames capable of connecting several themes when the texts justify such a synthesis.

A shared frame may encompass differences in priorities, intensity, or evaluation.

Conversely, several incompatible frames may coexist within the same group.

Do not force thematic homogeneity.

### Higher-level interpretation

A substantive qualitative interpretation may go beyond the literal wording of individual responses.

When several texts or themes support a meaningful configuration, reconstruct the broader concern, perspective, experience, or interpretive frame they express.

Such a reconstruction must remain grounded in the supplied texts and clearly identifiable as an analytical interpretation, not as a direct statement made by respondents.

Do not infer hidden motives, personality traits, moral qualities, psychological dispositions, social identities, or causal mechanisms that are not supported by the texts.

The goal is neither mechanical paraphrase nor speculative theorization.

### Internal diversity and tensions

Identify relevant disagreements, hesitations, minority positions, and contradictory or ambivalent statements.

Distinguish variations within a shared interpretive frame from the coexistence of substantially different frames.

An isolated but meaningful perspective may qualify the main interpretation without representing the entire group.

Do not suppress disagreements to create an artificially coherent profile.

### Evidence sufficiency

Adapt the scope and specificity of the interpretation to the number, diversity, and richness of displayed texts.

When only one or two texts are displayed, describe their expressed ideas cautiously without constructing an elaborate group-level identity, numerous artificial themes, or unsupported claims of consensus.

When no non-empty text is available, no qualitative textual profile can be established.

Do not invent themes, interpretive frames, coherence judgments, quotations, or text identifiers for an empty corpus.

### Role of the study context

Use the introduction to understand the research setting, the question asked of respondents, and the intended analytical focus.

The introduction is contextual information, not an additional observed response.

Distinguish empirical statements, evidence-grounded interpretations, and broader hypotheses that would require further investigation.

## Interpretation Rules

### 1. Identify the central thematic configuration

Identify the main recurring themes supported by the displayed texts.

Explain their substantive meaning, not merely the words that recur.

Where appropriate, connect related themes into a higher-level interpretation that captures what mainly structures the discourse.

Avoid constructing a broad profile from isolated expressions.

### 2. Assess the common interpretive frame

Determine whether the responses share an identifiable frame of meaning.

A shared frame does not require identical evaluations or complete agreement.

Distinguish a shared frame with internal variations from multiple coexisting or conflicting frames.

Do not automatically assume that membership of the same group implies a homogeneous discourse.

### 3. Examine internal diversity

Describe meaningful differences, tensions, ambiguities, reservations, and minority positions.

Preserve statements that qualify or contradict the central thematic interpretation.

A minority view may be substantively important even when it is not a recurring theme.

### 4. Assess coherence cautiously

Within-group coherence is a qualitative assessment of the displayed discourse, not a statistical measure.

Use the following interpretive distinctions:

- **Strong:** a clearly shared interpretive frame, with limited meaningful divergence.
- **Moderate:** a recognizable common frame accompanied by notable variation or qualifications.
- **Mixed:** several substantive frames, perspectives, or competing concerns coexist.
- **Weak:** no sufficiently identifiable common thematic frame emerges from the examined texts.

Distinguish low apparent coherence from insufficient evidence.

When the evidence is absent or too sparse for a defensible assessment, coherence should be treated as **not assessable**, subject to the output contract supported by the implementation.

Do not equate shared vocabulary with substantive agreement.

### 5. Construct an evidence-grounded synthesis

Produce an interpretation that explains what the texts collectively reveal about the discourse within the displayed group.

When justified, identify an overarching conceptual frame.

Make clear what is directly expressed, what is a thematic grouping, and what is a higher-level analytical interpretation.

Do not attribute unexpressed attitudes, motivations, or behaviors to respondents.

### 6. Preserve traceability

Support the principal interpretations using identifiable texts and themes.

Reference only supplied text IDs when the output contract requires representative or tension IDs.

A representative text illustrates an interpretation; one quotation alone does not demonstrate thematic prevalence.

Do not fabricate supporting examples or references.

---

## Local Scope

Interpret only the group displayed below.

Do not compare this group with other groups whose texts are not shown.

Do not infer the characteristics of other groups from the current group's responses.

The existence of multiple groups in the source data does not itself demonstrate differences in their discourses.

When the implementation returns several group-level interpretations together, they remain separate local interpretations, not an automatically generated cross-group synthesis.

---

# Data

[Automatically generated corpus information and selected textual evidence.]

Preserve:

- Original group identifier.
- Text identifiers and their links to source observations.
- The displayed text content.
- Corpus size and coverage information.
- Sampling information.
- Distinction between non-empty, distinct, and displayed textual responses.

The canonical textual evidence must remain separate from the selected interpretation input.

---

# Final Interpretation Requirements

Produce a substantive qualitative interpretation grounded in the displayed texts.

The interpretation should identify:

1. **Core textual profile** — the central meaning or configuration supported by the discourse.
2. **Main themes** — the principal recurring or substantively meaningful ideas.
3. **Interpretive frame and coherence** — what unifies the discourse, or why several frames coexist.
4. **Internal diversity** — meaningful variations, tensions, ambivalences, and minority positions.
5. **Traceable supporting evidence** — representative and contrasting texts, using original identifiers when required.
6. **Limits of interpretation** — insufficient coverage, sparse evidence, ambiguous language, or unresolved uncertainty.

The specificity of these conclusions must remain proportional to the available textual evidence.

Do not force a shared frame, multiple themes, or a coherence judgment when they are not supported.

No textual interpretation is justified for a group without non-empty textual responses.

Preserve the original introduction, analytical request, and group identifiers.

Use the existing NaileR output and parser contracts.

For `nail_textual()`, retain its structured group-level interpretation fields.

For `nail_textual_prep()`, retain its distinct short preparatory synthesis and reusable-output fields. Any changes to ID references, unsupported motives, or empty-group values require separate contract validation.

Do not silently introduce new output fields or allowed categorical values.

The output must remain compatible with the existing NaileR response-processing contract.

## Runtime dynamic sections

The implementation supplies the following runtime content without changing the approved doctrine:

- the user-provided `introduction`, preserved as contextual study information;
- the user-provided `request`, preserved as the analytical question;
- corpus coverage and sampling information;
- the selected `interpretation_input` and its exact text IDs;
- the canonical textual evidence separately stored in `textual_evidence`;
- the original group identifier and displayed text content;
- the existing output/parser contract for the selected route.

The distinction is:

`textual_evidence` (complete canonical registry) → `interpretation_input` (selected IDs/texts) → displayed evidence in the prompt.

A text not displayed because of sampling is not thereby absent from the corpus.

## Selection and evidence rules

The classic route converts text values to character, maps `NA` to empty strings, trims peripheral whitespace, and excludes empty texts from the registry. It preserves non-empty repeated texts as separate occurrences with `TXT%06d` identifiers based on source rows. Group-level metrics distinguish observations/rows, non-empty texts, distinct texts, and displayed texts.

`sample.pct` selects texts within each group; `seed` makes that selection reproducible while preserving the caller's RNG state. Sampling changes `interpretation_input` and visible prompts, not the canonical `textual_evidence`.

## Route-specific contracts

### `nail_textual()`

The classic route returns local prompts or local generation results according to `isolate.groups`, with a local-first architecture. Its structured profile contract contains the current fields:

- core textual profile;
- dominant themes;
- within-group coherence (`strong`, `moderate`, `mixed`, or `weak`);
- internal diversity;
- representative text IDs;
- tension text IDs.

The current parser and accessor contracts remain authoritative. Preview mode has no LLM response. `isolate.groups = FALSE` currently combines local prompts/results and does not perform an automatic global synthesis.

### `nail_textual_prep()`

The preparation route reuses the classic prompt workflow in isolated mode but has a distinct short structured output contract for later reuse. Its generated route parses preparation fields and may apply post-generation operations such as normalized deduplication, representative/tension verbatim selection, exclusion of short texts from representative attachment, truncation, and notable-expression extraction. These operations do not alter the canonical classic TEXTUAL registry.

The two route contracts must not be merged. In particular, future changes to identifiers, empty-group values, or allowed categorical values require separate parser and operational validation.

## Interpretive limits

The approved doctrine requires:

- literal and nuanced preservation of the displayed texts;
- traceability through supplied text IDs;
- distinction between occurrences, distinct texts, observations and individuals;
- cautious handling of sparse or sampled evidence;
- conceptual synthesis when several texts or themes support it;
- preservation of tensions, minority positions and contradictory statements;
- no unsupported motives, personality traits, moral qualities, social identities or causal mechanisms;
- no invented quotations, prevalence estimates or population-level proportions.

For empty groups, no qualitative profile is justified. The technical representation of missing/empty-group output remains an implementation contract to be validated separately.

## Local scope

Each prompt interprets only the displayed group. The current joint route remains local-first; it is not a global comparison or cross-group synthesis.
